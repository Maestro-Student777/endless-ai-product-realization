02 / Make the business understandable

What kind of company could this be?

First choose what work the business will do. Then compare a legal structure with an adviser. Researching and practicing do not require forming a company.

Legal structure

Sole proprietor · S.P.

One person doing business without a separate entity. Simple to begin, but business obligations can reach personal assets. A trade name does not create liability protection.

Ask:

“Which registrations, taxes and insurance would my actual activities require?”

Legal structure

Limited liability company

An LLC is a state-law entity that can have one owner. It can separate some business liabilities, but guarantees, personal conduct and insurance still matter. Tax treatment is a separate question.

Ask:

“For this property and operation, what protection and ongoing obligations would an LLC create?”

Business role

Property company

A PropertyCo holds real estate and may lease it to the operating business. It might be an LLC; the label itself is not a legal structure or a lodging permit.

Ask:

“Would separating the building from operations justify the financing, insurance and accounting costs?”

Business role

Holding company

A HoldCo owns interests in other companies. It can become useful with multiple assets or partners. Owning one small B&B does not automatically require three companies.

Ask:

“What concrete problem would an extra entity solve at my current stage?”

A practical sole proprietor starting checklist
  1. Describe the activity and choose the city/state before registering.
  2. Check whether a trade name/DBA filing is needed. In Minnesota, using a name other than your true full name generally requires an assumed-name filing; check the state's filing and publication instructions.
  3. Confirm tax registration, any EIN requirement, banking and bookkeeping with a qualified adviser.
  4. Before hosting guests, confirm parcel use, lodging/food approvals, fire/building requirements and insurance. Business registration alone does not authorize lodging.
  5. Compare personal exposure with an LLC before taking deposits, signing a property agreement or starting guest operations.

There is no education credential promised or granted here. Ask the licensing authority what qualifications and training apply to the specific operation.

04 / Solaris lodging research

Two paths. One grounded starting point.

The founder's own B&B

Compare an existing inn, a property conversion, and learning through operations first. Get financial records and local approvals before a purchase decision. Her business needs to work on its own economics.

Real example: BrickInn's acquisition story ↗

The Solaris partner idea

Begin by researching a preferred-rate agreement with one independent property. Later compare room allotments with release dates and guaranteed blocks. A guarantee creates a payment obligation even when rooms go unused.

No rooms are contracted and no contacts listed here are Solaris partners.

Long stays, “licenses to live,” and local rules

A contract label or LLC does not decide occupancy rights. In Minnesota, 30 days alone does not establish the lodging-tax exemption; the Revenue guidance specifies qualifying conditions. Tax treatment, tenancy rights, zoning, guest rules and work authorization are separate questions.

For each location, ask a local lodging attorney and the relevant agencies: Who is the operator? Who collects payments and taxes? What stay rules apply? What rights and notice requirements arise? A lodging agreement does not itself grant immigration or work permission.

    01 / Working founder profile

    A direction to test and refine

    This draft reflects the first conversation. It is a planning hypothesis that the founder can correct as she learns.

    Starting vision

    Explore a smaller bed and breakfast as the first ownership goal, with a focus on what its daily operations and guest experience would require.

    Long view

    Keep an adults only resort or larger hotel in a busier tourist area as a later possibility. There is no need to choose that second destination this month.

    Month One victory

    Know where to start: choose a practical first learning route, write down unknowns, and make a short next steps plan.

    Planning rhythm

    Make progress visible through one small action and one review each week. Record what was learned, what remains uncertain, and what to do next.

    02 / Month One preparation map

    Four weeks, four useful decisions

    Move from an idea to a reasoned starting point. These are research and planning exercises, not opening tasks.

    WEEK 1

    Define the first version

    Describe the B&B you might want: approximate room count, guest type, location possibilities, and what you want to do yourself.

    Output: a one paragraph concept and three questions.
    WEEK 2

    Learn the ownership routes

    Compare buying an existing B&B, converting a property, and starting as an operator or manager first. Note the skills each path requires.

    Output: a simple option comparison.
    WEEK 3

    Sketch the numbers

    List likely startup costs, ongoing expenses, room revenue assumptions, and questions for a lender or small business adviser. Use ranges until evidence improves them.

    Output: a first cost and funding question list.
    WEEK 4

    Choose the next step

    Review what the research changed. Pick one route to investigate further, one person to talk with, and a weekly planning habit.

    Output: a 30 day follow on plan.
    03 / Ownership path

    Separate the dream from the next decision

    A B&B and a large hotel involve different capital, operations, and teams. This path keeps the first decision small enough to investigate.

    1. Explore the concept. Choose a possible scale and guest experience to research.
    2. Compare routes. Existing business, property conversion, or experience in operations first.
    3. Check the constraints. Research location specific lodging rules, property costs, financing, and practical operating skills.
    4. Gather evidence. Talk to owners, operators, advisers, and potential guests; compare what you learn against your assumptions.
    5. Decide the next commitment. Make any purchase or business formation decisions only after the facts are clearer.

    Questions to answer before choosing

    ExperienceWhat hospitality work or training would help you learn the daily business?
    MoneyWhat size of project could you realistically investigate, and which funding questions need an expert answer?
    PlaceWhich region and guest type should you research first?
    PeopleWho could explain ownership, operations, and financing from lived experience?
    04 / Practice before commitment

    Run a small hospitality exercise

    Pick one exercise this week. The goal is to learn about the work and the numbers, then update the plan.

    Example: Imagine a four room B&B on a busy weekend. A guest checks in late, one room needs attention, and breakfast starts at 8 a.m. Write who handles each job, how long it takes, and what support would be needed.

    Three possible first experiments

    1. Interview an owner or manager about their first year.
    2. Draft a sample guest journey from booking to checkout.
    3. Make a paper budget with rough ranges and five questions to verify.

    Choose one. Keep observations separate from guesses.
